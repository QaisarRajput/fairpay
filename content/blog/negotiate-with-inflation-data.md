Most raise conversations are fought on vague terrain: what you feel you deserve, what your manager thinks is fair, what the company budget allows. Inflation data cuts through all of that by establishing an objective baseline.

Here is a framework for using CPI data in your next compensation discussion.

## Step 1 — Establish Your Baseline

Before the meeting, pull your salary history and identify the date and amount of your last raise (or hire date if you have not had one).

Compute your **break-even salary** — what you would need to earn today to have the same purchasing power as you did then:

`break_even = old_salary × (CPI_now / CPI_then)`

This number is not your ask. It is the floor below which any raise represents a real pay cut, regardless of what percentage it sounds like.

## Step 2 — Know Your Real Change

Calculate what has actually happened to your compensation in real terms:

`real_change (%) = (current_salary / old_salary) / (CPI_now / CPI_then) - 1`

If this is negative, you can open with: *"My nominal salary has increased X%, but adjusted for inflation over this period, my real compensation has declined by Y%. I'd like to discuss correcting that."*

This framing is factual, not emotional. It is harder to argue with than "I work hard and deserve more."

## Step 3 — Anchor to the Right CPI Series

Headline CPI is the most defensible benchmark in a negotiation because it is widely reported and publicly verifiable. But if your expenses skew toward a category with higher inflation — **shelter**, **medical care**, **transportation** — you can make the case for using that series instead.

Coming in with printed FRED data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics signals preparation and credibility.

## Step 4 — Separate Break-Even from Your Ask

Be clear in your own mind:

- **Break-even** = the salary that makes you whole against inflation. This is your floor.
- **Your ask** = break-even plus a premium for performance, market rates, additional responsibilities.

If you conflate these two, you risk anchoring too low. Closing the inflation gap first, then building a performance case on top of it, is structurally stronger.

## What to Expect

Not every employer will engage with CPI data. Some will redirect to "our standard increase is X%". That is useful information too — it tells you whether the company is willing to have a real compensation conversation or not.

In either case, having done this analysis means you walk into the room knowing exactly where you stand, and you leave it with a clear benchmark against which to evaluate any offer.
