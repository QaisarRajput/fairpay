The headline CPI number you see in the news is a **weighted average** across hundreds of goods and services. It is useful as a macro indicator. It is often useless as a personal benchmark.

Here is why, and how to read it more accurately.

## CPI Is a Basket, Not Your Basket

The Bureau of Labor Statistics constructs the Consumer Price Index by tracking prices across eight major categories:

1. Housing (shelter, utilities)
2. Transportation (vehicles, fuel, insurance)
3. Food and beverages
4. Medical care
5. Education and communication
6. Recreation
7. Apparel
8. Other goods and services

Each category gets a weight based on average consumer spending patterns. Housing alone accounts for over **40% of the headline CPI basket**.

If your spending skews toward food or medical care, the headline number almost certainly understates your personal inflation rate.

## Categories Move at Very Different Speeds

In 2021–2022, **used vehicle prices** spiked over 40% year-over-year due to supply chain disruptions. Headline CPI during that same window ran at around 7–9%. If you were in the market for a car, your experienced inflation was dramatically higher.

Conversely, **apparel prices** have been among the most stable components for decades. Someone who spends heavily on clothing and lightly on fuel may have experienced far less inflation than the headline suggested.

## Core vs. Headline CPI

- **Headline CPI** includes all items, including the volatile energy and food categories.
- **Core CPI** strips out food and energy to show the underlying trend.

Federal Reserve policy decisions typically reference *core* PCE (a related index), not headline CPI. When the Fed says inflation is under control, they may be looking at a measure that excludes the groceries and gas you actually buy.

## How to Apply This to Your Salary Situation

Rather than asking "was CPI up 3%?", ask which categories affected you most during the period your salary covers.

If you own a home or rent in a high-cost city, **shelter inflation** (which remained elevated long after goods prices cooled) is your most relevant benchmark.

If you commute by car, energy and vehicle costs matter more than apparel trends.
