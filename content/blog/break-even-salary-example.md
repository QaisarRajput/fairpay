Before you walk into a raise conversation, you need one number: your **break-even salary**. This is the exact compensation required to maintain the same purchasing power you had at your last review.

Here is how to calculate it and what to do with it.

## The Break-Even Formula

The math is simple once you have CPI data:

`break_even = old_salary × (CPI_now / CPI_then)`

This multiplies your previous salary by the ratio of current prices to past prices. The result is the salary that makes you no better or worse off in real terms.

## A Concrete Example

Suppose you earned **$80,000 in June 2021**.

The all-urban CPI (CPIAUCSL) for June 2021 was approximately **271.7**. By June 2024 it had risen to approximately **314.2**.

Your break-even salary for June 2024:

`$80,000 × (314.2 / 271.7) = $92,500`

If you are currently earning **$88,000**, you are $4,500 below the inflation break-even. In real terms, your purchasing power has declined even though your nominal salary rose $8,000.

Your **real change** is:

`($88,000 / $80,000) / (314.2 / 271.7) - 1 = -4.9%`

You received a nominal 10% raise but experienced a real 4.9% **cut**.

## Why This Matters in Negotiation

Most raise conversations start with: *what percentage increase is standard at this company?*

A better opening question is: *what salary is required just to keep me at the same real compensation as my last review?*

Break-even is not your ask — it is your **floor**. Anything below it represents a real reduction. Your ask should reflect genuine advancement above break-even.

## What to Do With Different CPI Series

The example above uses headline CPIAUCSL. But your personal inflation exposure may differ.

- If you spend heavily on **shelter** (rent or mortgage), use the shelter CPI series — it often runs higher than headline.
- If **medical costs** dominate your budget, the medical care CPI is more relevant.
- If you have a long car commute, **transportation CPI** might be your sharpest benchmark.

Running the break-even calculation against the series most relevant to your spending gives a more honest picture than headline CPI alone.
