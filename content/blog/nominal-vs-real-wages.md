You get a 5% raise. Inflation runs at 6%. You are, mathematically, poorer than you were a year ago.

This is the gap between **nominal wages** and **real wages**, and it matters more than most salary conversations acknowledge.

## What Nominal Means

Your **nominal salary** is the number printed on your offer letter. It does not account for what that money can actually buy. A salary of $80,000 in 2020 and a salary of $80,000 in 2024 look identical on paper — but they represent very different amounts of purchasing power.

Between January 2020 and January 2024, the Consumer Price Index for all urban consumers rose by roughly 19%. That means you would need approximately **$95,200 in 2024** just to match what $80,000 bought in 2020.

## What Real Means

Your **real salary** strips out the effect of inflation. It answers the question: *how much can I actually buy with this paycheck?*

The formula is straightforward:

`Real change (%) = (salary_new / salary_old) / (CPI_new / CPI_old) - 1`

If that result is positive, you gained purchasing power. If it is zero, you held even. If it is negative, your raise was a cut in real terms.

## Why This Gap Gets Ignored

Employers typically discuss compensation in nominal terms. A 4% raise sounds good — until you realize inflation ran at 5.5% that year. HR teams rarely volunteer the CPI-adjusted comparison.

This is not necessarily malicious. Nominal framing is simpler. But it can leave employees accepting raises that represent real reductions.

## How to Use This Knowledge

Before your next review, pull up CPI data for the period since your last raise and compute the break-even salary — the amount that would have kept your purchasing power exactly flat. That number becomes your floor in the negotiation, not your target.

- If your current offer is below break-even, you are being asked to take a real pay cut.
- If it matches break-even, you are treading water.
- Only if it exceeds break-even are you genuinely advancing.
